AI-First Accelerator Community Day: From Problems to Prototypes

261 officers, 47 solutions, 1 day Services Wing & SPSD officers at SEAB Multi-Purpose Hall

What happens when officers are given time to step away from day-to-day work and focus on one real problem with AI? At the AI-First Accelerator Community Day on 24 August, 261 officers did just that, creating 47 AI-assisted solutions across areas such as staffing, audit planning, email checking, invoicing, and subsidy monitoring.

The day was designed to move teams beyond talking about AI and into building with it. Officers worked through real work challenges, sharpened their problem statements, and developed early prototypes that could be tested and improved.

The day was opened by 2PS, who reminded officers that going AI-first is ultimately about people — building the confidence, capability and mindset to work differently, not just adopting new tools. Officers then got hands-on with ChatGPT Enterprise’s Projects and Skills, and rapid-prototyping tools Rabbit Deploy and Viber Studio, to bring their ideas to life.

One lesson stood out: the strongest ideas started with a clear understanding of the work problem, not with the AI tool. Teams that were able to define the problem well could move more quickly into designing and testing possible solutions.

Eight top-voted teams are now receiving further support to strengthen their prototypes ahead of the Gallery Walk on 30 September. For everyone else, the takeaway is just as relevant: before asking what AI can do, start by asking which part of your work is worth rethinking.

02

Prompt Royale x MOE Finals: 10 Minutes to Tackle Real School Challenges

⤢

What happens when HQ and school officers come together to tackle real school challenges, with just 10 minutes and AI at their side?

At the Prompt Royale x MOE Finals on 11 September, five finalist duos took on three challenges drawn from real school contexts — making sense of the problem, deciding what the AI needed to know, and refining their approach on the spot, using only four approved tools: Pair, Desk, AISAY, and Opus.

What made the finals interesting was the mix of perspectives in the room. HQ officers brought one view of the system, school officers brought the realities of day-to-day practice, and together they showed how AI becomes more useful when different experiences meet the same problem.

And if 10 minutes and four tools were enough to surface real ideas, it raises a bigger question: what could we achieve with more time to explore, test, and build around the challenges that matter most?

Kenny and Wee Lin emerged as this year's champions, with Ee Jun and Frederick taking first runners-up, and Eugene and Jin Wee placing second runners-up. But the bigger takeaway wasn't who won — it was what became possible when real problems, different perspectives, and AI came together.

3 things worth taking away

1
Confidence is built by doing.

For many finalists, prompting under pressure was new. But with each challenge, they became quicker at figuring out where to start, what to ask and what to do when the first attempt fell short. The more they tried, the more naturally it came.

2
Good prompting is about iteration, not perfection.

With just 10 minutes per challenge, there was no time to craft the “perfect” prompt. Teams had to start, see what came back, then refine, redirect and try again. The lesson? You do not need the perfect prompt to begin — you need to know how to build on what you get.

3
Knowing the work matters as much as knowing the AI.

Prompting skills only got teams so far. They still had to understand the problem, decide what mattered and judge whether the AI's response actually made sense. AI can help you do the work, but your context and judgement are what make the output useful.

03

What Could One Hour a Week Change?

One hour a week. Twelve weeks running.

↻ hover to replay

AI doesn't have to be something you cram in all at once. The 12-Week Learning Plan asks for just one hour a week — and builds learning straight into how you already work.

AI can feel like something you need to catch up on overnight. The 12-Week Learning Plan takes the opposite approach: start small, stay consistent, and learn through the work you're already doing.

Teams set aside just one hour a week to learn, experiment, and apply AI to a real work challenge — no separate course, no one-off workshop, just learning built into the rhythm of work.

Over 12 weeks, teams move from understanding the problem and using AI responsibly, to testing it on real tasks and rethinking the wider workflow. The goal isn't to know more about AI — it's to have improved one piece of work, and found a habit worth keeping.

Start with one problem. Give it one hour a week. See where the small changes add up. The plan is simple — the harder part is just making the space to begin.
