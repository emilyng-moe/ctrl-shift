Use Case of the Month

AI helped create it. Is it ready to go out?

An AI-generated draft, image or translation may look ready for publication. But before it reaches officers, parents or students, what should we check?

🧐 The scenario

Imagine a supplier submits a parent-facing guide for publication. The cover image was generated using AI, and a language model produced the first draft. The supplier has edited both, and the guide is now seeking approval.

The content may look complete, but is it accurate? Can the image be used? And who is responsible if something goes wrong?

These questions were explored in an AI knowledge workshop by the AI Programme Team, which focused on AI in everyday work and the risks, ethics and governance considerations surrounding publication and intellectual property (IP) work.

Here are six practical steps drawn from the workshop to help officers review AI-generated content.

1Classify2Decide oversight3Assess4Check5Name the owner6Record the decision
1
ClassifyWhat was AI used for?

First, understand how AI contributed to the work. Was it used to check grammar, summarise information or translate content? Or did it generate the first draft, artwork or other original material?

Also consider where the output will be used and who will see it. An internal working document carries different risks from an official resource published for parents or students.

2
Decide oversightHow much human review is needed?

The workshop outlines three levels of human oversight:

🧑‍💻
In the loopA person reviews and approves every output before release.
👀
Over the loopA person monitors outputs and intervenes when necessary.
🚦
Out of the loopOutputs reach the audience without human review.

For content published under the MOE name, human review is essential. AI-generated content can sound convincing while containing errors, so officers should verify facts, check translations and assess whether the material is suitable for its audience.

3
AssessCould the content raise ethical or disclosure concerns?

Consider four questions:

⚖️
FairnessWho is represented, and who might be left out?
🔍
TransparencyWould readers expect to know AI was involved?
🧾
AccountabilityWho is answerable if something goes wrong?
✍️
AuthorshipWho created the content, and does it matter?

Not every use of AI needs to be disclosed. Grammar checks and formatting, for example, may not require it. But realistic AI-generated images, synthetic voices or content where authorship and authenticity matter may warrant disclosure.

When in doubt, consider whether telling the audience would build trust, or seek advice.

4
CheckCan we use the output?

AI-generated content may resemble existing copyrighted work. Before publishing, check whether the output reproduces or closely imitates an existing work, and whether there is sufficient human creative or editorial contribution.

When working with vendors, ask whether AI was used, who owns the final output, where the content came from and who is responsible if copyright issues arise.

5
Name the ownerWho takes responsibility?

AI may contribute to the work, but accountability remains with people.

Identify the officer responsible for reviewing the final output and addressing any gaps. A vendor’s use of AI does not remove its responsibility for the work it delivers.

The key is to ensure someone can explain what AI contributed, what checks were carried out and why the content is ready to proceed.

6
Record the decisionIs it ready to proceed?

Before giving the green light, decide whether the work is ready, ready with conditions, or not ready yet.

✅ Ready🟡 Ready with conditions⛔ Not ready yet

Record any checks completed, missing information and actions required before publication. This creates a clear record of the decision and who is responsible for it.

Keeping human judgement at the centre

AI can help officers work more efficiently and explore new ways of doing things. But when its outputs are used in our work, we still need to ask the right questions, assess the risks and take responsibility for the final result.

The aim is not to avoid using AI, but to use it thoughtfully — with the right checks and human oversight in place. As the workshop reminds us: we cannot outsource judgement, but we can structure it.
